    Description

    This MicroSim builds the Fourier transform from one idea: wrap a signal around a spinning circle and see where its center of mass lands. A sine wave with period \(T_s\) (the shaded curve in the Cartesian Coordinates graph; its left edge is "now") is sampled and wound onto the Polar plot. Each sample becomes a point whose distance from the center is the sample value and whose angle makes one full turn every circle period \(T_c\). The Center of Mass panel repeats the wound curve, marks the average of all its points with a yellow dot, and prints that dot's distance from the center as \(|\text{COM}|\).

    • Circle period (0.10–2.00 s) sets \(T_c\). The blue marker shows \(T_c\) on both graphs, which span 0–2 s; the blue lines in the Cartesian graph mark each completed turn.
    • Signal period (0.1–1.0 s) sets \(T_s\). Resolution (10–300 points/s) is the sampling rate. Speed scales simulated time.
    • AutoPoll sweeps \(T_c\) from 0.10 to 2.00 s in 0.05 s steps at 20× speed. It records each strength once it stops changing; press it again to stop. Show Spin rotates the drawing instead of the pen.

    The Frequency Strength graph plots \(|\text{COM}|\) against \(T_c\), and its header names the strongest period and its frequency \(f_c = 1/T_c\). With \(t_n = n/R\) for resolution \(R\), the simulation computes

    \[|\text{COM}(T_c)| = \left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=0}^{M-1} x(t_n)\,e^{-i2\pi f_c t_n}\right|,\]

    a sampled, averaged form of the Fourier transform \(X(f) = \int x(t)\,e^{-i2\pi f t}\,dt\) at \(f = f_c\). For \(x(t) = \sin(2\pi t/T_s)\) it settles at \(1/2\) when \(T_c = T_s\) and falls to zero otherwise.

    This MicroSim supports Chapter 6: Fourier Analysis Fundamentals.

    Lesson Plan

    Learning Objectives

    By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

    1. Explain how winding a signal around a spinning circle turns it into a center of mass whose distance from the origin measures the strength of one frequency
    2. Predict that the center of mass stays about 0.5 from the origin only when the circle period equals the signal period, and keeps returning to the origin otherwise
    3. Interpret the AutoPoll frequency-strength graph to find the period and frequency of a sine wave
    4. Relate the center-of-mass average to the Fourier transform integral \(X(f) = \int x(t)e^{-i2\pi f t}\,dt\)
    5. Identify an alias peak caused by a low resolution (sampling rate) and compute where it appears

    Target Audience

    College undergraduates in a first signals and systems course, after complex numbers and before the discrete Fourier transform. Plan 20–30 minutes.

    Prerequisites

    • Complex numbers in polar form and Euler's formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\)
    • Period and frequency of a sinusoid, \(f = 1/T\)
    • The center of mass (average position) of a set of points

    Activities

    Activity 1: A mismatched winding (5 min). Keep the defaults (\(T_s\) = 0.50 s, \(T_c\) = 0.30 s, Resolution 300, Speed 1.00x) and press Play. The wound curve becomes a three-lobed rose that is balanced around the center. \(|\text{COM}|\) is about 0.15 after 0.5 s and 0.08 after 1 s, then returns to 0.000 at 1.5 s, the first time that both 3 signal periods and 5 turns of the circle are complete. It returns to zero every 1.5 s after that.

    Activity 2: A matched winding (5 min). Press Reset, set the circle period to 0.50 s and press Play. The positive and the negative half of every cycle now land on the same side of the center (a negative radius flips a point to the opposite side), so the curve is a single circle that passes through the center. \(|\text{COM}|\) settles at 0.50 (0.498 after 1 s). Now set \(T_c\) = 1.00 s. The curve becomes a four-petal rose, and \(|\text{COM}|\) is back to 0.000 after every whole second. Check Show Spin: the red dot on the polar plot now moves straight up and down in step with the red dot at the left edge of the Cartesian graph.

    Activity 3: Detect the frequency (8 min). Press Reset, then AutoPoll, and wait for the sweep to reach 2.00 s (about 13 s). One stem stands out at 0.50 s with a strength of about 0.51, and the header reads 0.50 s = 2.00 Hz. Its neighbors at 0.45 s and 0.55 s reach only about 0.11, and the rest stay below about 0.1. Set the signal period to 0.8 s and run AutoPoll again (about 22 s): the peak moves to 0.80 s, which is 1.25 Hz.

    Activity 4: An alias (7 min). Set Resolution to 10 pts/s and the signal period to 0.3 s, so a 3.33 Hz sine is sampled only 3 times per period (the Cartesian graph shows a jagged three-point wave). Run AutoPoll. Two stems of about 0.5 appear: at 0.30 s (3.33 Hz) and at 0.15 s (6.67 Hz). They are equally strong, so the header may name either one. The 0.15 s stem is an alias at \(f_s - f = 10 - 3.33 = 6.67\) Hz. At 10 samples per second, winding at 6.67 Hz cannot be told apart from winding at \(-3.33\) Hz.

    Assessment

    1. A sine wave is wound with \(T_c = T_s\). Where does its center of mass end up, and why? (Answer: about 0.5 from the center. Each turn of the circle covers exactly one cycle, and the negative radius flips the other half-cycle onto the same side of the center, so nothing cancels. The wound curve is a single circle through the center whose center of mass is 0.5 away.)
    2. AutoPoll shows a single peak at \(T_c\) = 0.25 s. What is the frequency of the signal? (Answer: \(f = 1/0.25\ \text{s} = 4\) Hz.)
    3. With \(T_s\) = 0.5 s and \(T_c\) = 0.3 s, why does \(|\text{COM}|\) return to exactly zero every 1.5 s? (Answer: 1.5 s is the least common multiple of the two periods. It holds 3 whole signal periods and 5 whole turns, and over such a span the sum of \(\sin(2\pi t/T_s)\,e^{-i2\pi t/T_c}\) is zero whenever \(1/T_c \ne 1/T_s\) (and no alias is involved). This is the orthogonality of sinusoids.)
    4. Explain why the AutoPoll stems at 0.45 s and 0.55 s are not zero when the signal period is 0.5 s. (Answer: AutoPoll watches each \(T_c\) for only a few signal periods. At \(T_c\) = 0.55 s, \(|\text{COM}|\) is still 0.49 after 1 s and 0.30 after 3 s, and reaches 0 only at 5.5 s, so nearby frequencies need a long observation to tell apart. This is spectral leakage, and frequency resolution is about \(1/T\) for observation time \(T\).)
    5. At Resolution 10 pts/s, a 3.33 Hz signal produces a second peak at \(T_c\) = 0.15 s. Explain it. (Answer: sampling at \(f_s\) = 10 Hz makes winding at 6.67 Hz identical to winding at \(6.67 - 10 = -3.33\) Hz. A real sine has a component at \(-3.33\) Hz, so the alias appears at \(1/6.67 = 0.15\) s.)

    Common Misconceptions

    "The horizontal axis of the strength graph is frequency." It is the circle period \(T_c\), so frequency \(f_c = 1/T_c\) decreases to the right. The header converts the peak to hertz (0.50 s = 2.00 Hz). Compare the 0.80 s peak in Activity 3 with the 0.50 s peak: the slower signal appears farther right.

    "Only the exact matching period gives a large center of mass." Close periods look matched for a while. Set \(T_s\) = 0.5 s and \(T_c\) = 0.55 s and press Play: \(|\text{COM}|\) starts near 0.49 and decays only slowly (0.42 at 2 s, 0.17 at 4 s), reaching zero at 5.5 s. Telling close frequencies apart takes a long observation.
